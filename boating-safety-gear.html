Boating Safety Gear: Essential Kit

Tides turn small mistakes into emergencies: a falling tide strands you, a running ebb builds the inlet, and slack water is never slack for long. This is the safety kit that keeps a bad tide from becoming a bad day.

Tidal water punishes the unprepared

Most boating emergencies aren’t dramatic — they’re a dead battery at low water, a channel that moved since last season, a fog bank over a tidal rip. Our tides and currents guide covers planning around the water’s moves; this page is the gear that bails you out when the plan meets reality.

The gear

Inflatable fishing PFD

The PFD you’ll actually wear: slim, cool, and out of the way until the CO2 cartridge does its job. Belt-pack and suspender styles both beat the bulky vest sitting in the locker — because the best PFD is the one on your body.

Handheld VHF marine radio

When the phone has no bars and the fog rolls in, channel 16 is your lifeline: weather broadcasts, hailing nearby boats, and the Coast Guard. Waterproof, floating models are the standard now — a radio that sinks is a radio you don’t have.

Visual distress signals

Flares, an orange flag, and a signal mirror — the kit the Coast Guard requires and the one you hope to never use. Check expiration dates every spring; expired flares are the most common safety violation afloat.

Marine first aid kit

Hooks, knives, sun, and slippery decks — a marine first aid kit handles the injuries boating actually produces, in a box that survives the bilge. Restock the sunscreen and seasickness tablets; they expire faster than the bandages.

Throwable flotation cushion

Required equipment that doubles as a seat cushion, and the first thing you throw to someone in the water. Keep it instantly reachable — buried under the anchor rode, it’s just decoration.

Compact marine fire extinguisher

Fuel, batteries, and enclosed spaces: the fire triangle lives aboard every powerboat. Mount a Coast Guard–approved extinguisher where you can grab it in seconds, and check the gauge each season.

Portable bilge pump

Electric pumps fail when the battery does. A manual bilge pump — or even a sturdy bailer — moves water when nothing else will. If you’ve ever watched the tide come through a leaky fitting, you know why this matters.

Anchor with rode

On tidal water, the anchor is a safety device: it holds you off the rocks when the engine quits and keeps you in the channel while you sort out a problem. Size it for your boat plus the current you’ll anchor in — tidal current loads an anchor harder than wind.

File the float plan, check the tide

Gear is the backup; judgment is the plan. Tell someone where you’re going, check your tide chart before you launch, and respect the ebb at the inlet. Then go enjoy the water — that’s what the kit is for.
