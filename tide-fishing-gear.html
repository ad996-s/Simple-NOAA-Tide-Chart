Tide Fishing Gear: What to Bring

Fishing the tides is a timing game, and the right gear turns a good tide into a great trip. This is the kit that matters most — every item earns its place because the tide demands it.

Why tide fishing needs its own kit

Tide anglers deal with moving water, changing depths, slippery structure, and sessions that start before dawn or run past dusk — see our guide to the best tides for fishing for the timing side. The gear below is chosen for exactly those conditions: stuff that keeps you safe, mobile, and fishing while the water moves.

The gear

A tide watch

Your phone dies; a tide watch doesn’t. Watches with a built-in tide graph (Casio’s are the classics) show the tide stage at a glance, so you always know whether the water is pushing in or draining out — no signal required.

Saltwater spinning combo

One versatile 7-foot medium-heavy spinning combo handles most inshore tide fishing — redfish, snook, stripers, flounder. Sealed reels (Penn’s Battle series set the standard) shrug off the salt spray that comes with fishing moving water.

Braided line

Braid’s thin diameter cuts tide-driven current better than mono, so your lure stays in the strike zone instead of bowing downstream. Spool 20–30 lb braid with a fluorocarbon leader and you’re set for most inshore work.

Tackle bag, not a box

Tide fishing means moving — following the water up a beach or along a jetty. A soft tackle bag (Plano makes the go-to) carries more, weighs less, and doesn’t crack when you set it on riprap.

Wading boots

Falling tides expose flats, oyster bars, and reef — prime fishing, brutal footing. Proper wading boots with grip save ankles and keep stingrays at a shuffle’s distance.

Headlamp

The best tides don’t care about daylight: dawn patrol, dusk bites, and summer grunion runs all happen in the dark. A bright headlamp with a red mode keeps your hands free and your night vision intact.

Polarized sunglasses

Sight-fishing tailing redfish or reading a flat is impossible without polarization. They also cut the glare that hides current seams and bait schools — essentially a fish-finder for your face.

Stainless pliers

Salt water eats cheap tools. Stainless pliers pull hooks, cut braid, and crimp leaders trip after trip. Rinse them after every outing and they’ll outlast your reel.

Put it together

Pack this kit, then check the water before you go: pull up your local tide chart, aim for the moving water around the tide changes, and fish the structure the tide exposes. Tight lines.
