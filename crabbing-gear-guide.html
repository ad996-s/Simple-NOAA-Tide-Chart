Crabbing Gear Guide

Crabbing is one of the cheapest ways into saltwater harvesting — a trap, some bait, and a tide chart. Here’s the gear that actually matters, and the tide timing that makes it work.

Gear matters less than timing — almost

Ask any crabber: the tide decides the catch more than the trap does. Slack water around the tide changes is prime, since crabs feed hardest when the current eases — see our crabbing and tides guide for the full timing playbook. That said, the right gear keeps you legal, efficient, and crabbing instead of untangling.

The gear

Box crab trap

The classic two-door box trap is the workhorse for blue crab from a dock or pier: crabs walk in, can’t walk out. Look for vinyl-coated wire that resists rust and escape rings sized for your state’s regulations.

Collapsible crab pot

Short on space? Collapsible pots fold flat for transport and fish nearly as well as rigid traps — ideal for kayakers and anyone working from a small boat.

Crab gauge

Keeping an undersized crab is a fine waiting to happen, and wardens do check. A simple gauge gives you an instant legal-or-release answer on every crab. Cheap insurance — buy two.

Trap rope and buoy kit

Dropping a trap without a marked buoy is donating it to the bay. A leaded rope-and-buoy kit keeps your trap retrievable and your float visible to boat traffic.

Bait bags and bait box

Chicken necks and fish heads work, but they work better contained: bait bags keep bait in the trap longer and make rebaiting a ten-second job instead of a mess.

Grip gloves

Blue crab claws and barnacle-covered rope are unforgiving. Grippy, quick-dry gloves save your hands on every pull — you’ll handle dozens of crabs on a good tide.

Bucket with lid

A lidded 5-gallon bucket is the crabber’s live well: keeps the catch contained and alive on the trip home. Bring a second for trash — bait scraps add up.

Waterproof headlamp

Some of the best crabbing happens at night on a slack tide, and sorting a trap in the dark without light is a good way to meet a claw. A waterproof headlamp is non-negotiable for after-dark drops.

Time it right

Rig up, then fish the clock: drop traps on the incoming tide and pull them around slack water, when crabs feed hardest. Check your local tide chart, mind your state’s size and season rules, and enjoy one of the best dollar-per-pound fisheries there is.
